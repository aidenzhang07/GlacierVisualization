What is a Glacier?

Educational background

A glacier is a persistent, moving body of dense ice formed from the compaction of snow over many years. They form when more snow accumulates each winter than melts each summer. Over decades this compresses into firn, then into glacial ice — ice dense enough to flow downhill under its own weight. Glaciers are among Earth's most sensitive climate indicators: they integrate temperature and precipitation signals over decades, making them invaluable long-term climate records.

What is Mass Balance?

Mass balance is the net annual change in a glacier's ice volume — the difference between snow/ice gained (accumulation) and lost (ablation). Measured in m w.e. (metres water equivalent) to account for density differences between fresh snow, firn, and glacier ice. A negative mass balance means the glacier lost more mass than it gained — it is shrinking.

How Glaciers Are Measured

Glaciological method

Field teams install ablation stakes drilled into the ice surface. Each year they measure how much of each stake has emerged — this gives net surface lowering. Snow pits in the accumulation zone measure winter snowpack depth and density. Together these give annual point measurements that are averaged over the glacier area.

Geodetic method

Repeat satellite DEMs (Digital Elevation Models) are differenced to measure volume change over multi-year periods. Increasingly, this uses ICESat-2 laser altimetry (±0.03 m accuracy) or TanDEM-X radar. Geodetic surveys calibrate the glaciological record for density assumptions and instrumentation drift.

Remote sensing

Multispectral satellite imagery (Landsat, Sentinel-2) maps glacier outline changes annually. The NDSI (Normalized Difference Snow Index) distinguishes ice from rock and debris. This feeds the GLIMS database — the same source used to generate the map you came from.

Radar ice thickness

Ground-penetrating radar (GPR) surveys emit radio pulses that reflect off the glacier bed. Two-way travel time × wave velocity in ice gives bed depth. Airborne surveys (NASA Operation IceBridge, ITGC) extend this to remote glaciers. GlaThiDa archives ~3.9 million such thickness measurements globally.

Causes of Recession

Global mean temperature has increased ~1.2°C since pre-industrial times. Mountain regions are warming at 1.5–2× the global mean rate. For every 1°C of warming, the ELA rises approximately 150 m — committing glaciers below that line to irreversible loss.

Temperature rise

Warmer summers extend the melt season and raise the rain/snow transition altitude. The Arctic and high mountain regions are warming 2–3× faster than the global average.

Albedo feedback

As white ice surface melts, darker rock, meltwater, and debris are exposed. These absorb more solar radiation, accelerating further melting in a self-reinforcing cycle.

Precipitation shifts

Even where total precipitation is unchanged, warming converts snowfall to rainfall at lower elevations — reducing winter accumulation and accelerating early-season melt.
